Can AI and humans make “real music”?

A Korean Animated Feature Made with AI and Music —
AIGENBERG’s LYRA

The strength of Korean animation lies in its in-house production capabilities. In step with the industry’s growth, the Korean government supports both production and overseas expansion. Since 2023, it has expanded per-project support, and it has incorporated new production technologies such as AI into its policies to support experimentation. This matters for international producers, too: by partnering with a Korean studio, they can build projects that draw on both Korea’s production capabilities and its support programs.

The first company we meet is AIGENBERG, an AI‑native studio that has produced more than 100 projects across film, animation, VFX, XR, and music for more than a decade. In Toronto, it will present LYRA, selected this year for the AI Feature Animation Production Support Program of the Korea Creative Content Agency (KOCCA). Set in 2041 Los Angeles, where AI music dominates the industry, LYRA follows a human musician and a robot as they set out to make “real music.” We hear about the pipeline that takes the film from production through distribution.

THE INTERVIEW

with Sam‑yeong Jung, CEO, AIGENBERG

Your team has a decade of experience and more than 100 completed projects across film, animation, VFX, XR, exhibitions, and music-driven content. How has that experience shaped your approach to AI‑native production today?

Sam‑yeong Jung: Our team has spent the past decade working in advertising and exhibition production. Through projects involving Samsung, the National Museum of Korea, BTS, and Disney, we’ve built our expertise in directing and managing productions from start to finish, bringing everything from concept development to post-production together into one cohesive piece.

That experience carries directly into how we work with AI today. AI allows us to explore more creative possibilities, refine every detail more relentlessly, and keep improving the quality of our work.

As a hybrid studio with AI built into our production process, we’re expanding what’s creatively possible while keeping strong storytelling and thoughtful direction at the heart of everything we make.

 

What role does AI play in making LYRA, and which parts of the process involve human musicians? How do you make sure the technology doesn’t compromise the film’s artistic vision?

Sam‑yeong Jung: We use AI to assist with script analysis and visual production. Our original character designs and artwork are created by our in-house animators, and their drawings form the visual foundation for the film’s AI‑assisted production. For the music, we’re working with music director Taehoon Lee to shape the score around the characters’ emotions and stories. We’re also preparing to collaborate with a human vocal artist.

The most important part of how we’re making LYRA is our pre-scoring approach. We establish the timing and rhythm of the dialogue and music first, then build the scenes around that flow.

The emotional arc and rhythm designed by people form the backbone of our direction, and AI-generated scenes are selected and refined to serve that intent.

Our guiding principle is simple: when it comes to conveying the characters’ emotions and story, the director’s vision always leads.

 

How has being selected for KOCCA’s 2026 AI Feature Animation Production Support Program affected LYRA’s production timeline and scale?

Sam‑yeong Jung: The biggest benefit of being selected is that we now have a solid foundation to keep moving forward with a feature-length production.

With funding from the program and our own resources, we’re producing a film expected to run around 90 minutes or longer, with a release or premiere planned for summer 2027.

Our in-house team handles the core production work, while we collaborate with specialists in areas such as music and voice acting.

Right now, our main focus is on maintaining consistency and quality in the characters, emotional storytelling, and music throughout the entire film.

LYRA is set in 2041 Los Angeles, where AI music dominates the industry, and follows a human musician and a robot who set out to make “real music.”

You’ve had hands-on experience in both production and distribution, including distribution through KT, one of Korea’s largest telecom companies, a theatrical release at CGV, Korea’s largest cinema chain, VOD on Korean streaming platforms, and an OST release. How do you plan to apply that experience to LYRA’s global distribution strategy?

Sam‑yeong Jung: Through the theatrical release, streaming distribution, and OST release of The Prime Pattern: The Echo, we’ve learned to think about how a film reaches its audience from the very beginning of production.

We’re taking the same approach with LYRA, preparing it to reach audiences by connecting theatrical releases, streaming, and music.

For international markets, we’d like to work with partners who understand their local audiences and develop localization and distribution strategies together.

We especially hope the music will live on beyond the film and grow interest in the story and its characters.

“When it comes to conveying the characters’ emotions and story, the director’s vision always leads.”

Sam‑yeong Jung

CEO, AIGENBERG

 

You’re developing LYRA alongside the VR game Orphan Code. What kinds of international sales agents, investors, and distributors are you hoping to meet in Toronto?

Sam‑yeong Jung: We’re looking for partners who connect with our stories and the worlds we’re building, and who want to work with us to find the best ways to reach audiences in different markets.

For LYRA, we’re interested in meeting international sales agents, distributors, and streaming partners who understand the appeal of a feature-length animated film that brings together music and sci-fi.

For Orphan Code, we’re looking for publishing and platform partners who can help bring its immersive, story-driven VR experience to players around the world.

Beyond the individual success of these two projects, we’d also love to connect with investors who share our long-term vision for The Prime Pattern, the larger IP universe that brings both projects together.
